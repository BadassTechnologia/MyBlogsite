On 6 October 2026, Francis Halzen won the Nobel Prize in Physics for his role in building the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and discovering high-energy neutrinos from space. To see how unusual that achievement was, picture a telescope with no mirror, buried deep under the South Pole. Its job is to catch brief flashes of light from particles that usually pass straight through Earth. [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announcement](https://www.kva.se/en/news/the-nobel-prize-in-physics-2026/)

Francis Halzen was born in 1944 in Tienen, Belgium. He studied physics at what is now KU Leuven, receiving his master’s degree in 1966 and his PhD in 1969. He joined the physics faculty at the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1972. A theoretical physicist by training, he went on to lead the international effort to build IceCube. [KU Leuven biography](https://nieuws.kuleuven.be/en/content/2026/ku-leuven-alumnus-francis-halzen-nobel-prize-physics) · [UW–Madison profile](https://www.physics.wisc.edu/directory/halzen-francis/) · [Niels Bohr Institute](https://nbi.ku.dk/english/news/happening-at-nbi/2026/nobel-prize-in-physics-goes-to-icecube-pioneer-francis-halzen/)

## What is a neutrino, and why look for one?

A neutrino is a particle with no electric charge and very little mass. It rarely interacts with matter. That means a neutrino can cross a cloud of gas, pass through a planet, and keep going. The same property that makes it hard to catch makes it useful to astronomers. Neutrinos can carry information out of regions where light is absorbed. And because they have no electric charge, magnetic fields do not bend their paths as they do for charged cosmic rays. A neutrino’s arrival direction can therefore give scientists a clue about where it came from. [Scientific background from the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://www.kva.se/app/uploads/2026/10/nobel-physics-2026-scientific-background-2.pdf)

## What did Francis Halzen do?

Halzen helped turn a practical idea into a working observatory. If a neutrino almost never interacts with matter, give it an enormous amount of matter to travel through. Deep Antarctic ice could serve both as the material a neutrino might strike and as a clear medium through which the resulting light could travel.

An earlier project called AMANDA tested the approach by placing light sensors in South Pole ice. Halzen submitted an IceCube proposal to the US National Science Foundation in 1999 and led the collaboration that built the larger detector. The final sensor cable went into the ice in December 2010. Halzen led the effort, and the IceCube Collaboration built the instrument and analysed the data together. [AMANDA research collaboration](https://iihe.ac.be/2026-nobel-prize-in-physicsfrancis-halzen-the-icecube-experiment-and-the-iiheulb-vub-30-years-of-active-involvementand-major-advances-in-understanding-the-universe/) · [American Physical Society](https://www.aps.org/about/news/2026/10/aps-winner-2026-nobel-physics) · [IceCube construction overview](https://icecube.wisc.edu/science/icecube/)

## How does IceCube detect a particle it cannot see?

IceCube monitors about one cubic kilometre of ice with 5,160 optical sensors spread across 86 vertical cables. The sensors sit between 1,450 and 2,450 metres below the surface. Here is what happens when the detector catches an event. [IceCube detector facts](https://icecube.wisc.edu/about-us/facts/)

1. **A neutrino interacts in or near the ice.** On rare occasions, it strikes an atomic nucleus and produces other particles.
2. **Charged particles produce a flash.** If they travel faster than light travels through ice, they emit a faint glow called Cherenkov radiation. They can do this because light travels more slowly in ice than in empty space.
3. **The sensors record the pattern.** Researchers use the timing and brightness of the flashes to estimate the event’s energy and the direction the neutrino came from. Some events leave long tracks that give a clearer direction, while others produce compact bursts of light. [IceCube detector overview](https://icecube.wisc.edu/science/icecube/) · [Nobel scientific background](https://www.kva.se/app/uploads/2026/10/nobel-physics-2026-scientific-background-2.pdf)

## What did the 2013 discovery show?

Finding a neutrino in IceCube does not automatically mean it came from deep space. Cosmic rays strike Earth’s atmosphere and produce neutrinos there, too. The team had to estimate how many events it would see from these and other atmospheric sources before it could identify a signal from beyond the atmosphere.

In 2013, the IceCube Collaboration reported 28 candidate events from data collected between May 2010 and May 2012. The researchers expected about 11 background events. The excess and the properties of the events gave strong evidence for high-energy neutrinos from cosmic sources. The study established that a population of these particles reaches Earth. Pinpointing which objects produced them was the next challenge. [IceCube Collaboration’s 2013 paper in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1242856)

## Where do high-energy cosmic neutrinos come from?

In 2017, IceCube detected a high-energy neutrino that appeared to come from the direction of TXS 0506+056, a galaxy with a jet pointing close to Earth’s line of sight. Telescopes saw the galaxy brighten in gamma rays around the time of the alert. The team reported the association in 2018, and a separate analysis found evidence of earlier neutrino activity from the same direction. In 2022, IceCube reported evidence of neutrino emission from another active galaxy, NGC 1068, also called Messier 77. These findings link some neutrinos to specific objects, while the sources of much of the overall signal remain uncertain. [IceCube research highlights](https://icecube.wisc.edu/science/research/) · [TXS 0506+056 data release](https://icecube.wisc.edu/data-releases/2018/07/icecube-data-from-2008-to-2017-related-to-analysis-of-txs-0506056/) · [NGC 1068 study and data](https://icecube.wisc.edu/data-releases/2022/11/evidence-for-neutrino-emission-from-the-nearby-active-galaxy-ngc-1068/)

## Why the discovery matters

When an observatory detects a neutrino and telescopes see light from the same part of the sky, scientists can compare two kinds of evidence about one event. The light shows what the object emits at different wavelengths. The neutrino can reveal interactions involving fast-moving particles in places that light may struggle to escape. Using these signals together is called **multimessenger astronomy**, and it gives researchers a way to investigate how galaxies accelerate particles to extreme energies. [Nobel scientific background](https://www.kva.se/app/uploads/2026/10/nobel-physics-2026-scientific-background-2.pdf)

## Conclusion

Halzen’s contribution was to help turn a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice into a working telescope for neutrinos. IceCube then showed that high-energy neutrinos from space reach Earth in numbers scientists can measure. The 2026 Nobel Prize recognises that achievement. The next question is which objects produce most of these particles and how they give them so much energy.


## A closer look at the science and what IceCube makes possible

### Why did Halzen need a cubic kilometre of ice?

A neutrino detector has two jobs. It needs enough matter for a rare interaction to happen, and it needs a way to see the light that follows. Deep South Pole ice can do both. A small detector can register a neutrino, but collecting enough high-energy events to compare with the atmospheric background calls for a much larger target. Halzen helped take the idea from the earlier AMANDA experiment to IceCube, where thousands of light sensors monitor a volume large enough to study those rare events as a group. The collaboration also uses the sensors near the edge of the detector to identify particles that enter from outside. That helps separate those events from interactions that start inside the array. [Nobel scientific background](https://www.kva.se/app/uploads/2026/10/nobel-physics-2026-scientific-background-2.pdf)

A cubic kilometre is the volume of a cube 1,000 metres on each side. Multiplying 1,000 by itself three times gives one billion cubic metres. At roughly 920 kilograms per cubic metre, that volume of ice has a mass of about **920 million metric tonnes**. The sensors occupy a tiny fraction of that volume. It is the amount of ice they monitor that gives the detector so many opportunities to catch an interaction. [IceCube detector facts](https://icecube.wisc.edu/about-us/facts/)

### How do scientists read the light patterns?

A muon is a charged particle related to the electron. When an interaction produces a muon, it can travel far enough to leave a long light pattern called a **track**. Researchers use the times at which sensors recorded light to work backwards to the muon's path and estimate where the neutrino came from. Other interactions make a compact shower of particles called a **cascade**. A cascade contained in the array can give a better measure of the energy deposited, although a long track generally gives a clearer direction. When particles leave the array, some of the original neutrino's energy can escape measurement. [Nobel scientific background](https://www.kva.se/app/uploads/2026/10/nobel-physics-2026-scientific-background-2.pdf)

### What does the 2013 result look like in numbers?

The study's central estimate for the atmospheric background was 10.6 events. Dividing the 28 observed candidates by 10.6 gives about **2.6 times the expected background count**. That ratio describes the size of the excess. To judge whether random variation could explain it, researchers also had to account for uncertainty in the background and consider properties such as event energy and direction. Two events deposited more than one petaelectronvolt, or PeV. One PeV is one quadrillion electronvolts, about **0.00016 joules**. That energy is small on an everyday scale but remarkable when carried by one subatomic particle. [2013 research article](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1242856) · [IceCube's PeV-event paper](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.111.021103)

### How does this help astronomers today?

When fast protons or atomic nuclei collide with matter or radiation near an astronomical source, they can produce short-lived particles that decay into neutrinos. Charged cosmic rays can be deflected by magnetic fields, while neutrinos travel without that deflection. Finding neutrinos from the direction of a galaxy gives astronomers evidence they can use to investigate whether the galaxy accelerates protons or nuclei. It also points telescopes towards a source they can study with other instruments. [Nobel scientific background](https://www.kva.se/app/uploads/2026/10/nobel-physics-2026-scientific-background-2.pdf)

IceCube can send real-time alerts when it detects a promising event. A telescope can then look in that direction while the source may still be active. The same detector can also look for a burst of lower-energy neutrinos from a stellar collapse in our galaxy. Individual events in such a burst are generally too faint for IceCube to reconstruct. Instead, IceCube looks for a brief rise in the combined sensor counts and can share the signal through the SuperNova Early Warning System. That gives astronomers a chance to coordinate observations of the explosion as it unfolds. [IceCube real-time alerts](https://icecube.wisc.edu/science/real-time-alerts/)
