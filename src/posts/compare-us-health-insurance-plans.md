A health plan that costs $120 less a month can still leave you paying more for the year. The difference might come from one medication, an imaging appointment that meets the deductible, or a specialist who works inside one network but outside another. Compare the plans in that order of consequence: check whether you can get the care you need, work out what a full year would cost under several scenarios, then decide what level of financial risk your household can absorb.

This guide focuses on U.S. Marketplace plans and uses invented figures to show the arithmetic. Employer coverage can be compared using the same framework, but employer contributions, payroll deductions, enrollment rules, and plan documents can differ. Medicare and Medicaid have their own rules and need a separate comparison.

## Start with the documents for the exact plan

Collect the monthly premium for your household, the Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC), the provider directory, and the prescription formulary. Keep a note of the plan name, network, coverage year, and exact plan identifier. Insurers can sell several plans with similar names. An office that takes the insurer can still be outside the network for the particular plan you are looking at.

The SBC gives you a standard format for comparing cost sharing and lists common medical events. The plan’s full contract or evidence of coverage gives more detail about exclusions, definitions, and coverage conditions. The formulary explains which prescription drugs are covered and how they are classified. Use the four pieces together. A deductible alone tells you almost nothing about whether your preferred pharmacy can fill a particular medication at an affordable price.

For an employer plan, obtain the employee cost for your actual coverage tier, such as employee plus spouse or family coverage. If the deduction is given per paycheck, multiply by the number of deductions in the plan year. A $150 deduction taken 26 times would be $3,900 per year. Some employers use a different deduction schedule, so verify how many payments apply before comparing the result with a Marketplace premium quoted monthly.

## Check which plans can deliver your care

Make a short list of the services you are likely to use in the next plan year. Include specialists you already see, the hospital or health system you rely on, prescriptions you fill regularly, and any planned procedures or monitoring. Mark items that would be difficult to replace. A network that does not work for your ongoing treatment can make an otherwise attractive price irrelevant.

### Verify providers by location and plan

Use the insurer’s directory for the specific plan, then confirm with the insurer or provider when a relationship matters to your decision. Ask whether the clinician participates at the location you intend to visit and whether the practice is taking new patients on that plan. A listing for one office is not enough if the appointment would take place at a different location. For a procedure, ask how the facility, clinician, and associated services are contracted. Keep the date and the source of the answer.

Check the referral and prior authorization rules separately. A referral is generally a request or direction from another clinician, often your primary care clinician. Prior authorization is a step the insurer may require to approve coverage for some services. Which one applies depends on the plan and the service. If you have a planned scan or procedure, ask who is responsible for submitting authorization and what documentation the plan requires.

A directory search can also uncover access issues even if you are willing to change clinicians. Look for a backup primary care office, check practical travel times, and ask about appointment availability. A list of specialists across a metropolitan area may be less useful than two clinics you can actually reach that have appointments in the timeframe you need.

### Check prescriptions by exact product and pharmacy

Write down the drug name, strength, formulation, and refill frequency. Find the entry in the plan’s formulary and check the tier, whether the deductible applies, and whether coverage is subject to authorization, step therapy, or quantity limits. A plan may place a tablet and an extended-release version on different terms. An insurer’s pharmacy tool may show different prices across preferred and other network pharmacies.

If a drug is expensive or difficult to substitute, verify the rules directly. Ask whether a separate prescription deductible applies and whether the plan uses a copayment or a percentage of the allowed amount. Some specialty drugs come with dispensing restrictions, so ask which pharmacy can fill them. Record a plausible cost per fill and multiply by the expected number of fills for the year. Keep the drug cost separate in your worksheet so you can see when it changes the conclusion.

## Calculate what the plan costs in a normal year

Start with the **annual premium**, which is the amount paid to keep coverage. Then add estimated **member cost sharing**, the amount you pay for covered services as you use them. That second part can include deductible spending, copayments, and coinsurance. Avoid adding the entire deductible to every scenario. It represents a threshold that applies to certain services, and you might use too little care to meet it.

The **allowed amount** is the amount on which the insurer bases payment for a covered service. Under a simple deductible-first rule, you might pay the allowed amount until the relevant deductible is met. After that, you might pay a percentage called coinsurance. A copayment is a fixed dollar amount for a covered service under the terms of the plan. Some copays apply before the deductible and some do not. Record the rule for each service.

Suppose one imaging claim has an allowed amount of $900, you have $400 left on the applicable deductible, and the service then has 20% coinsurance. In this simplified example, the first $400 satisfies the deductible. The remaining $500 is subject to coinsurance, giving a member cost of $400 plus $100, or $500. A service with a special copay or a separate facility charge could work differently, so apply the example only to an item that follows the stated rule.

Summarize your use in three cases. A low-use case includes the recurring care you expect even in a quiet year. A typical case includes credible additions such as follow-up visits and routine testing. A high-use case tests the effect of a serious covered illness or an expensive course of care. Use round estimates when you lack precise prices and label them as estimates. The goal is to understand which assumptions matter enough to verify before enrolling.

## Worked example with two hypothetical plans

Both plans cover the same clinicians and drugs in this example. The premium shown is the amount paid by the member, and the figures are invented for illustration.

| Item | Plan A | Plan B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Monthly premium | $380 | $500 |
| Annual premium | $4,560 | $6,000 |
| Individual medical deductible | $2,800 | $900 |
| Coinsurance after deductible for the modeled services | 20% | 15% |
| Individual in-network out-of-pocket maximum | $7,000 | $4,500 |

Assume all modeled claims are eligible in-network services subject to the same deductible and coinsurance. Assume there are no separate prescription costs or copays in this example. At $600 in allowed claims, each plan charges $600 to the member because each deductible exceeds that amount. Plan A costs $5,160 for the year and Plan B costs $6,600.

At $4,000 in allowed claims, Plan A charges $2,800 plus 20% of $1,200, or $3,040. Added to $4,560 in premiums, that is $7,600. Plan B charges $900 plus 15% of $3,100, or $1,365. Added to $6,000 in premiums, that is $7,365. Plan B costs $235 less in this case even though it charges $1,440 more in premiums over the year.

The result changes because the higher premium buys lower cost sharing under the example’s rules. For allowed claims between $900 and $2,800, Plan A’s member cost is the amount of the claims, while Plan B charges $900 plus 15% of allowed claims above $900. In that interval the total costs become equal at about $2,594 in allowed claims. You do not need to solve a formula when choosing a real plan. The lesson is to test enough points to see where the lower-premium plan stops saving you money. Add rows in a spreadsheet for $1,000, $2,500, and $4,000 of modeled care and compare the totals.

Real claims rarely follow a single rate. If a plan offers primary care before the deductible, calculate those visits using the stated office-visit rule and estimate imaging or hospital care separately. Also model the order of services for a household expecting care early in the year. Even when two plans have similar estimated annual totals, one may require more cash before you have had time to save for it.

## Understand the high-use benchmark

A useful benchmark for one covered person is the annual premium plus the applicable in-network out-of-pocket maximum. In the example, that is $11,560 for Plan A and $10,500 for Plan B. It describes the combined cost if the member reaches the plan’s maximum for eligible covered care. Premiums themselves do not count toward the maximum. Noncovered services and parts of some out-of-network bills can add costs beyond the benchmark. Check the contract for what counts toward each limit.

Compare family rules carefully. Some plans track individual and family deductibles or limits, and the figures that apply to one member can differ from the figures that apply when several family members get care. Ask how spending is accumulated, including whether an individual can satisfy a deductible separately and how the family limit is reached. A household expecting maternity care for one member faces a different pattern from a family with ongoing treatment spread across several members.

You can also separate financial risk from practical affordability. A deductible may technically fit within a yearly budget but still be difficult to pay if several claims arrive at the start of the plan year. Compare your available reserve with the amount you might owe in the first quarter. If the higher-premium plan leaves you with a smaller up-front exposure and better access to recurring care, that can be more valuable than the lowest estimate in a low-use year.

## Compare subsidies on the same basis

The premium displayed on a general comparison page can differ from the amount due after eligibility is determined. Use the same household and income assumptions for all plans you compare. Premium tax credits can reduce the cost of eligible Marketplace coverage. If a household qualifies for cost-sharing reductions, it generally must enroll in a Silver Marketplace plan to receive those reductions. Check the specific plan variant and its SBC so you are comparing the deductible and copays that actually apply to you.

An employer contribution also changes the effective price. Use the amount the worker pays for the relevant coverage tier and check how payroll deductions are presented. If an employer contributes to a health account or offers another benefit, track that amount separately and verify its terms. The ability to spend account money on an eligible expense is a different question from the cost the insurer will assign to the claim.

## Make the decision and record your assumptions

Start by removing any plan that fails a requirement you cannot easily change. Next, compare the yearly cost under your quiet, typical, and high-use cases. Check whether an expensive drug, a hospital stay, or an early-year procedure could reverse your preference. If two plans still look close, revisit the providers and appointment availability you actually need.

Record your final comparison in a compact decision note. Include the plan identifier, premium used, deductible and maximum, the provider confirmations you relied on, and the assumptions that drive the result. Save the SBC and formulary for that year. When a bill or coverage question comes up, you can go back to the terms you compared instead of trying to reconstruct your decision from a marketing page.
