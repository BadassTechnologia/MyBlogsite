A detector can count evidence of newly made gold even when no piece of gold reaches the detector. In a study published in Physical Review C in 2025, the ALICE collaboration used signals from particles emitted by lead nuclei to measure a reaction that can leave behind gold nuclei. At peak rates, CERN estimated that thousands of gold nuclei formed each second at the ALICE interaction point. The experiment yielded a measurement of nuclear reactions, with quantities far below anything you could weigh on a laboratory balance.

To understand what ALICE achieved, follow three quantities through the experiment. The number of protons determines the element. The number of neutrons helps determine the isotope and its stability. The number of detector signals, once corrected for the way the experiment records them, helps determine how often a reaction happens. Keeping these quantities separate resolves much of the apparent mystery around making gold in a laboratory.

## What changes when lead becomes gold?

A lead nucleus has 82 protons, and a gold nucleus has 79. If a lead nucleus loses three protons and the remaining fragment stays together, that fragment has the nuclear charge of gold. The mass number, which counts the total number of protons and neutrons, depends on how many neutrons were lost as well. Chemists use the element name for every isotope with the same proton count, even when some of those isotopes decay quickly.

Take lead-208, the isotope in the ALICE beams. It starts with 82 protons and 126 neutrons. If a reaction removes three protons and one neutron, simple bookkeeping gives a fragment with 79 protons and 125 neutrons, giving a mass number of 204. The element is gold. If a hypothetical reaction removes three protons and eight neutrons, the arithmetic instead gives gold-197, the stable isotope found in natural gold. These examples show why a three-proton count can identify a charge-changing channel without establishing that it yielded stable gold-197.

The distinction also explains why ordinary laboratory chemistry cannot carry out this particular transformation. If you dissolve gold and then recover the metal from a solution, the gold nuclei already existed in the starting compound. Chemical reactions can rearrange atoms and change how electrons are shared or transferred. Creating gold from another element calls for a change to the nucleus itself.

## How does a near miss change a nucleus?

The Large Hadron Collider can accelerate beams of fully stripped lead ions. A fully stripped ion has lost the electrons that ordinarily surround its nucleus. The beam particles carry the positive charge of their protons and travel in opposite directions around the accelerator. At an interaction point, the beams pass through each other. Some ions collide through their constituent matter. Other pairs pass close enough for their electromagnetic fields to interact even when the nuclei themselves do not overlap.

Physicists call an encounter in the latter geometry an ultraperipheral collision. In one way of analyzing it, the moving electric field of an ion acts as a source of photons that can transfer energy to the other nucleus. A photon that transfers enough energy can leave that nucleus excited. An excited nucleus can shed particles as it relaxes. This process is known as electromagnetic dissociation.

You can think of it as a two-stage event. First, a passing ion supplies energy through its electromagnetic field. Second, the excited nucleus changes into a lighter fragment by emitting nucleons or other particles. The field supplies the opportunity for a reaction. Nuclear structure and reaction dynamics determine which particles leave and what survives. A given encounter may leave the beam nuclei intact, and many dissociation events produce fragments of elements other than gold.

Ordinarily the word collision suggests contact. In this context it describes an encounter whose measurable consequences can occur at a separation where the two nuclei do not overlap. That distinction matters because a direct nuclear collision can produce a much more complicated spray of particles. Studying electromagnetic dissociation gives the collaboration a way to isolate a class of nuclear changes associated with near misses.

## What exactly did ALICE detect?

ALICE used detectors called zero-degree calorimeters. A calorimeter registers energy deposited by particles. Zero-degree instruments sit in the forward direction, where suitable collision fragments can continue near the line of the original beam. The experiment used separate instruments for forward neutrons and protons. Neutrons carry no electric charge, while protons can be deflected by accelerator magnets, so the two types of particle take different paths toward their detectors.

The researchers used the proton signals to distinguish events with zero, one, two, or three detected protons, in samples defined with neutron emission. The energy deposited by particles helps separate those groups statistically. A signal consistent with two protons belongs to a different charge-removal channel from one consistent with three protons. The measured distributions also need treatment for detector response and the probability that particles reach the instruments. A count of raw signals alone would mix detector behavior with the physics of the reaction.

Starting from a lead nucleus, the simple charge sequence for losing zero through three protons is lead, thallium, mercury, and gold. That sequence describes the charge of the residual fragment in the corresponding proton-loss picture. It helps interpret the channels, but the 2025 paper measures forward proton and neutron emission and uses models to relate those observations to the heavier residual nuclei. Equivalent comparisons require attention to other charged fragments, to what the detectors accept, and to the event definitions used in the analysis.

Most importantly, the detectors did not catch a stock of gold atoms. The gold conclusion comes from the relationship between detector measurements and the inferred residual fragments. To describe the result accurately, say that ALICE measured particle emission associated with gold production and estimated the corresponding production. That phrasing preserves the achievement and tells a reader what can actually be traced back to the instruments.

## Why was counting the emitted particles scientifically useful?

A central output of a particle-collision study is often a cross section, a quantity with units of area that describes the probability of a specified reaction under the conditions of the experiment. For a given process, expected event yield depends on the cross section and the integrated luminosity, a measure of the collision opportunities accumulated over time. Detectors then record only a subset of the resulting events. The analysis accounts for the way events were selected and how effectively the instruments recorded them.

This is different from estimating the abundance of gold in a mineral sample. A sample analysis begins with a material that can remain in a container. ALICE began with beams and looked for signatures of individual interactions. The useful question for the collaboration was how frequently each emission channel occurred. A strong measurement can test how well a model predicts the relative frequency of an event emitting one proton versus several.

There is a practical accelerator reason to care about those probabilities. A fragment that changes its mass or electrical charge can follow a different path through magnets from the lead ions the accelerator is set up to carry. When those fragments hit equipment or the inside of the beam pipe, they deposit energy. Understanding which fragments are made and how often can help accelerator researchers model where beam losses occur. The value of the measurement lies in the underlying reaction data even if the total gold output is tiny.

When comparing two headline numbers, ask what each number counts. A measured cross section for a named emission channel, a model estimate of the number of gold nuclei, and a peak rate at one collider experiment answer separate questions. A peak rate is especially easy to misuse. It describes conditions at particular times and locations. Multiplying it by every second in a calendar year would assume continuous operation at the same peak, with matching beams and conditions.

## How much gold did the collider make?

CERN estimated that across the four major experiments during the LHC's second run, about 86 billion gold nuclei were generated, corresponding to roughly 29 picograms. A picogram is one trillionth of a gram. Because those numbers describe estimated production, they do not mean a 29-picogram sample could be retrieved from a known container.

You can check the scale with a back-of-the-envelope calculation. Use 197 grams per mole as an approximate conversion, and use approximately 6.022 × 10²³ nuclei per mole. One nucleus then corresponds to roughly 3.27 × 10⁻²² grams. Multiplying that figure by 86 billion gives about 2.8 × 10⁻¹¹ grams, or about 28 picograms. The small difference from CERN's rounded estimate is unsurprising given the approximations. Using 197 as a representative mass in this calculation does not establish that the fragments were gold-197.

Even a single milligram would require around 3 × 10¹⁸ gold nuclei at the same representative mass. Compare 3 × 10¹⁸ with 8.6 × 10¹⁰ and the scale gap is around 35 million fold. This compares produced mass with a milligram under an idealized assumption that every nucleus could be counted toward that mass. Any practical attempt to obtain usable material would face additional questions about collection, survival, and isotopic composition.

## Why couldn't scientists collect the gold?

The newly formed fragments are moving in an accelerator, and their electrical charge and mass differ from those of the original lead ions. The machine's magnets guide particles according to their momentum and charge. A fragment can therefore depart from the intended beam orbit and strike a collimator or another part of the machine. CERN describes the gold nuclei as short lived in the accelerator environment because collisions with material break up the fragments. That physical loss is separate from radioactive decay, which depends on which isotope was formed.

Producing a usable sample would mean solving several different problems. You would have to direct the fragments to a collector, slow them down without destroying them, and determine which isotopes survive. You would also need to accumulate enough atoms to measure and handle. ALICE's experiment was designed to learn about particle emission and beam fragmentation. Its signal came from suitable forward particles reaching detectors, so there was no need to collect each gold nucleus for the measurement.

This explains why the result can be both experimentally credible and commercially irrelevant. The research team can measure how frequently a microscopic process occurs, while the accelerator is a poor route to macroscopic metal. Economic comparisons would have to include the equipment, how often the necessary beams run, and the yield of material actually recovered. Given that CERN reported a picogram-scale production estimate before collection losses, a market price comparison would add little to the scientific explanation.

## How to read similar lab-made gold claims

First, identify the starting material and look for the proton count. An account of gold nanoparticles formed from a solution may describe making a particular shape or size of particle from an existing source of gold. A transmutation study should identify the starting isotope and show how the residual nuclear charge can reach 79. That is the point where the different studies become comparable.

Next, identify the evidence. A direct measurement of an isotope has a different meaning from a count of emitted particles that supports a model-dependent estimate. Both can be scientifically valuable. For the ALICE study, the clearest order is to look at the measured proton and neutron signals, then at how the analysis infers the various heavy fragments. If an article jumps straight from a photograph of the detector to a claim about recovered gold, check whether it explains that intermediate step.

Finally, check whether the amount refers to nuclei produced, stable atoms remaining, or material collected. Write down the units and the time span. For example, compare a production rate in nuclei per second with a total count over a collider run only after checking which locations and running conditions the two numbers represent. A rate at one interaction point and an integrated estimate across four experiments can both be correct while answering different questions.

## What to take from the experiment

Use proton bookkeeping to explain the element change, and use the research paper to explain what the instruments measured. ALICE started with lead nuclei, measured forward particle emission from electromagnetic encounters, and inferred gold production in the channel associated with removing three protons. The small mass and the fate of the fragments explain why this was an accelerator measurement rather than a way to supply gold. If you want to examine the quantitative result further, start with the paper's event definitions and compare them with the particle paths shown in the collaboration's experiment summary.
