The first ferry is an hour away, the path is dark, and a trail of footprints stops at the last lantern. That is enough to start a one-minute story.

Use [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) to develop a story, refine the narration, and plan what viewers should see. Then use [Golpo AI](https://video.golpoai.com/) to turn the approved material into a narrated video. The handoff is a script you can read aloud, plus a short scene plan. You can copy the material between the two tools and review it at each stage.

This guide builds a one-minute story about a lantern keeper who finds footprints in fresh snow. The same workflow works for a classroom story, a product demonstration, or a short explainer. The example includes an opening, a clue, a reveal, and an ending so you can see how each part translates into a scene.

## 1. Decide what the viewer should follow

Write a brief before opening either tool. Name the audience, the format, the approximate running time, and the one question that keeps someone watching. For this example, the audience is general, the format is a narrated one-minute story, and the question is who left the tracks at a lantern nobody has visited since the storm.

Give the story one protagonist and one immediate goal. The lantern keeper needs to relight the lamps before the first ferry arrives. The footprints interrupt that plan. Since the audience knows why the keeper is outside, following the tracks feels like a decision with a cost. She is using time she had set aside for lighting the lamps.

Choose one visual detail that can return later. In this example, the keeper carries a brass key. She finds that the same key fits an old lantern control box. That gives the final action a connection to something viewers saw in the opening. Put the key in the first scene description so it has a chance to appear on screen before the reveal.

## 2. Develop the outline in [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/)

Ask for a short sequence of events. If you already know which choices you want to keep, give those to [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) as constraints. If the ending is still open, ask it to list two plausible explanations for the footprints. Choose an ending before drafting the narration.

```text
Help me outline a one-minute narrated story for a general audience.
The main character is a lantern keeper who must relight a snowy
footpath before the first ferry arrives. She carries a brass key.
She finds fresh footprints at a dark lantern.
Use four beats: the task, the clue, the reveal, and the final action.
Give each beat one visible event. Keep the action in one location.
End with an explanation the viewer can understand from the clues.
```

Read the four beats in order. In each beat, the viewer should be able to answer what changed. If two beats both say the keeper notices something strange, combine them. Give the remaining beat a distinct action, such as opening the control box or following the tracks past the last lamp.

Try giving [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) a short piece you wrote if you want the narration to use your own rhythm. Ask for specific edits, such as shortening the first two sentences or making the last sentence easier to say aloud. A targeted revision is easier to check against your intent than a request to rewrite the whole story.

## 3. Write and time the narration

Start with about 110 to 130 words for a calm one-minute read. Treat that range as a planning assumption. Different speakers, pauses, and pronunciations change the timing. At 120 words per minute, 120 words take one minute before any extra pauses. If you want five seconds of silence across that minute, budget about 110 spoken words at the same rate.

Here is a sample script. Read it aloud with the pauses you intend to use.

> The first ferry would arrive in an hour. Mara took her brass key and went to light the path from the landing. Snow covered every step except for a trail of small footprints leading to the last lantern. She followed them to a metal box half buried in a drift. Someone had scraped the snow away from its lock. Her key still fit. Inside was a lever painted red, with a label that read “reserve lamps.” Mara pulled it. One by one, lights appeared along the path. A fox slipped from beneath the box and disappeared into the snow. By the time the ferry arrived, the path was bright.

The key and the fox solve different parts of the story. The fox explains the tracks. The key lets Mara act on what she finds. If your render makes the fox seem responsible for opening the box, change the narration or the scene order. For example, show the fox after Mara pulls the lever, and describe the exposed lock as an effect of the wind instead of saying someone scraped the snow away.

Record a timing pass on your phone or read the script while running a timer. Mark any line where the image needs time to catch up. If the ending feels rushed, remove a sentence or lengthen the target. The generator’s selected duration is a setting. The time a real listener needs to understand the story is an editorial decision.

## 4. Plan what each scene should show

Write one visual action beside each section of narration. Keep names, colours, and objects consistent. A recurring visual description can help you spot when the character or setting changes between shots.

| Approximate time | Story beat | What to show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 14 seconds | The task | Mara holds a brass key near a row of dark lanterns on the snowy path. |
| 14 to 28 seconds | The clue | Small tracks cross otherwise smooth snow and stop near a metal box. |
| 28 to 46 seconds | The action | Mara opens the box, reads the reserve-lamp label, and pulls a red lever. |
| 46 to 60 seconds | The ending | A fox slips away as lights come on and the ferry approaches. |

Use the table as production notes. In an input field intended only for narration, paste only words the narrator should speak. Keep timing notes, visual directions, and labels in separate controls when your plan and mode provide them. If a label matters, check the resulting frame. Narration can deliver the information if text inside the generated image is hard to read.

## 5. Generate a first cut in [Golpo AI](https://video.golpoai.com/)

[Golpo](https://video.golpoai.com/) documents two input approaches. **Prompt Mode** is suitable when you want the system to develop an explanation from your brief. **Script Mode** is the better starting point when you have already revised the narration and want the generation based on that script. Review what the selected mode does to the words you enter. Controls and limits vary by plan.

Start with the shortest practical test. Use one script, one visual style, and the frame format that matches where the video will run. Keep the approved script in a separate file so you can compare it with the output. If the tool presents a script review step before generation, check names, numbers, and the final sentence there.

If you can provide visual instructions separately, specify the details a viewer needs to recognise across shots. For the example, tell the generator to use the same keeper, brass key, path, and metal box. Describe the red lever once and refer to it consistently. Avoid assigning a different visual action to every phrase. Viewers need time to recognise the clue and how it matters.

## 6. Review the video before sharing it

First, watch the cut with the sound on. Check that you can hear the first line, the narration matches the approved script, and names are pronounced clearly. For an educational video, verify dates and technical terms against your sources. Ask another person what happened in the story after one viewing. If they missed the reveal, look at what the storyboard showed immediately before it.

Next, watch with the sound off and read any captions. Check that the action is understandable, the key visual details stay consistent, and text fits on a small screen. For the lantern story, look at the box and the fox in particular. A strong cut gives each a clear moment on screen. If the tool does not offer the caption or edit controls you need, finish that work in your usual editor.

Keep a record of what you changed. If the first cut opens with a wide shot that hides the key, try a closer opening. If the narration runs long, cut a line from the script before generating again. Changing one issue at a time makes it easier to judge what improved and helps avoid spending credits on repeated full-length tests.

## Fix the weak spots in a first cut

A first cut can be technically complete while the story still feels hard to follow. Start with three questions. Can you see what the protagonist wants? Can you recognise the clue when it appears? Does the ending connect the clue to what happened? For the lantern story, watch the first fifteen seconds as a separate clip. If it shows only snow and a figure walking, viewers may miss that the character is racing to light a path before a ferry arrives. Add the deadline to the first narration line and show the dark lanterns in the opening shot.

Then review the handoff between the clue and the reveal. The tracks should remain visible long enough for the viewer to notice their size. The control box and the fox need separate visual moments. If the fox appears before the viewer understands the lever, the shot may solve the mystery of the tracks while leaving the main action unclear. Let the lamps come on and then reveal the fox. The order gives the audience time to understand both events.

You may be able to repair the issue with a shorter line or a revised cut. For example, “The tracks stopped at the reserve lamp box” gives the viewer one clear thought. A longer sentence that also explains the key and describes the condition of the box puts several ideas over one image. Read the line with the scene playing. If you find yourself wanting to pause the video to explain what happened, make the script or the image simpler.

## Adapt the workflow to an explainer

Keep the four-beat structure when the video explains a process. Give the opening a problem, show a concrete example, explain the mechanism, and end by applying it. An explainer about parcel tracking could begin with a customer wondering why a package has stood still for a day. Show a parcel being scanned at a depot, explain that tracking records an event when a scan happens, and end with what someone can check before contacting support. Confirm procedural claims against the carrier’s own help pages before recording them.

For classroom material, ask [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) to flag terms a learner might not know. Review the result and choose the one or two terms worth defining in narration. Put other detail in the captions or accompanying notes. Then use [Golpo AI](https://video.golpoai.com/) to test whether the visual example is clear enough to carry the explanation. After a first viewing, ask a learner to explain it back using a new example. If they can repeat a definition but cannot apply it, revise the example before spending time polishing the animation.

The same check helps with a promotional video. Specify the problem the audience faces and show the action the product supports. Verify demonstrations and product claims before generating the narration. A polished cut will carry whatever story you gave it, including a claim you have not checked.

## Use the workflow for a developer demo or game tutorial

Start from a working build, a tested command, or a recorded playthrough. Give [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) the verified sequence and ask it to turn the steps into narration for a specific viewer. A developer learning the feature needs the inputs, expected output, and one common failure. A player learning a mechanic needs to see the action, the visual cue that confirms it worked, and the rule that determines when to use it. Show those details in the opening scene plan.

For a developer demo, make a simple review table with the narration line, the exact interface label or command, and the evidence that it works. If the video explains an API response, preserve the structure of a verified example and remove real tokens, account IDs, and customer data. Keep version information beside the script so you can tell when a later product change makes the video stale. A generated explanation can guide the viewer, while your own screen recording can show the exact code or menus.

For a game tutorial, record a clean example of the mechanic from the player’s perspective. Use [Golpo AI](https://video.golpoai.com/) to generate the setup or a simple animated explanation. Add the gameplay recording when the viewer needs to see the real timing, controls, or HUD. Test the cut with someone who has never played the game and ask them to reproduce the action. If they get the timing wrong, replace the vague instruction with the cue they should watch for.

## Conclusion

Build the story around one question and four visual beats. Use [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) to revise the script, time it aloud, and prepare a scene plan. Use [Golpo](https://video.golpoai.com/) to generate a short first cut and check it against the approved narration. Once someone can follow both the clue and the payoff in one viewing, prepare the final export in the format your audience will watch.
