A flash of light can make a neuron fire. The discovery that made this possible started with a single-celled alga.

On October 5, 2026, Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, and Georg Nagel shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics. The work began with a question about how a microscopic alga responds to light. It led to a way for neuroscientists to change the activity of selected nerve cells at a chosen moment and measure what happens next.

## What is optogenetics?

Optogenetics uses light-sensitive proteins to control the activity of selected cells. In neuroscience, researchers arrange for the cells they want to study to make a protein that responds to light. A flash can then change the flow of charged particles across those cells’ membranes. That change can make a neuron more or less likely to send an electrical signal, depending on the protein being used.

The term combines optics, which supplies the light, and genetics, which helps determine which cells respond. Timing comes from the light source. Selectivity comes in part from which cells make the protein and where the light reaches. Those two controls work together. The light can reach neighbouring cells, but the direct response is concentrated in the cells that carry the light-sensitive protein.

## What did the three laureates contribute?

**Peter Hegemann** studied how the single-celled green alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* responds to light. For this alga, sensing light helps it navigate as light conditions change. The research raised a question that was also useful to neuroscientists. What kind of protein could detect light and produce an electrical response quickly enough to affect the cell’s movement?

**Georg Nagel** helped establish how the algal proteins worked. In a 2003 study, Nagel, Hegemann, and colleagues described **channelrhodopsin-2**, usually shortened to ChR2, as a channel that opens directly in response to light and lets positively charged ions pass. The study showed why the protein might work outside the alga. Its light-sensitive component and ion channel were part of the same protein.

**Karl Deisseroth** helped turn that property into a tool for neuroscience. A 2005 paper by Edward Boyden, Feng Zhang, Ernst Bamberg, Nagel, and Deisseroth showed that mammalian neurons could respond to brief pulses of light when they made channelrhodopsin-2. The team recorded electrical activity and demonstrated control on a millisecond timescale. The Nobel Prize named three laureates, and the research papers show how many collaborators contributed to the work.

## How can light make a neuron fire?

A neuron maintains an electrical voltage across its membrane. The voltage depends on the distribution of charged particles inside and outside the cell and on which ions can cross the membrane. Ion channels are proteins that let particular types of ions cross under certain conditions. Some open in response to a change in voltage. Channelrhodopsin-2 responds to light.

ChR2 contains a light-absorbing molecule called retinal. When it absorbs a photon, the molecule changes shape. That change alters the protein and opens a route through which positive ions can move. In a resting neuron, the resulting current can make the inside of the cell less negative. This change is called **depolarisation**. If the cell reaches the threshold for an action potential, it sends a brief electrical signal along its length.

The flash and the electrical response are two separate events. A flash opens the light-sensitive channel. The neuron’s electrical machinery determines whether the resulting current is enough to make it fire. The response can vary between cells, and light can change activity without producing a spike every time.

Light can also be used to reduce activity. Some proteins move charged particles in ways that make an action potential less likely. That gives researchers two complementary questions to ask. What happens if a group of neurons becomes active at this moment? And what happens if its usual activity is reduced?

## What did the early experiments show?

### 1. Establish that the algal protein can carry an electrical current

The 2003 team needed to determine whether ChR2 itself could open a pathway for ions in response to light. They used electrical recordings to detect currents as the protein responded. They also studied isolated pieces of membrane. Finding a light-evoked current there made it possible to distinguish a channel that opens directly from a process that depends on an additional signalling pathway elsewhere in the cell.

This distinction mattered for the next experiment. A protein that directly connects light to an ion current offers a more straightforward way to move the effect from an alga to a different type of cell. The new cell still needs the right conditions for the protein to work, but it does not need the alga’s full machinery for responding to light.

### 2. Test whether the same principle works in neurons

The 2005 study asked whether mammalian neurons could turn that current into an electrical response useful for research. The team arranged for the neurons to make ChR2, delivered brief flashes, and recorded what the neurons did. The recordings showed that light could drive action potentials on a timescale close to the timescale that neurons use to communicate.

A millisecond is one-thousandth of a second. A 10-millisecond interval is therefore 0.01 seconds. To see why this matters, imagine two signals arriving 20 milliseconds apart. A tool whose effect takes several seconds would be difficult to use to test a question about which signal came first. A tool that acts on the millisecond scale lets scientists design a test that addresses the interval directly. These numbers illustrate the difference in timescale. They are independent of the exact pulse settings used in a particular study.

### 3. Turn a cell response into a test of a circuit

Once researchers could change selected neurons’ activity, they could ask how a circuit responds. Imagine recording a group of neurons every time an animal makes a turn. You might see activity just before the turn, but that timing alone cannot establish what the activity does. It could help initiate the movement, reflect a decision made elsewhere, or carry information about something the animal sensed.

A manipulation adds a second line of evidence. Researchers can change the activity around the moment when a turn is likely and compare the outcome with a suitable control. If turning changes, that supports a role for the targeted cells under the conditions of the experiment. The result can then be checked against recordings and against other ways of testing the same circuit.

## Why is optogenetics important?

By combining cell selection and timing, optogenetics helps connect observations at the scale of single neurons to questions about an entire circuit. Conventional electrical stimulation can activate nearby cells and nerve fibres. With optogenetics, researchers can use differences between cell groups to narrow which cells respond directly. That is useful when two kinds of neurons are intermingled and may serve different functions.

The method also helps separate two ideas that are easy to confuse. If activating a circuit can cause a behaviour under certain conditions, the activity may be sufficient to produce that result in the experiment. If reducing the circuit’s activity prevents the behaviour, it may be necessary under those conditions. One test does not automatically establish the other. An animal may have several routes for producing a similar action, and activity created in the lab might differ from the pattern normally seen during the behaviour.

There are also limits to what a single experiment can establish. Light spreads in tissue. Different groups of cells can make different amounts of the protein. And activating one group can change the activity of cells downstream. Scientists need controls and recordings to work out how much of the measured effect comes from the intended change. The ability to choose a target makes a causal test possible, but the strength of the conclusion still depends on the design and the evidence.

## What does it mean for medicine?

The immediate value is in research. Understanding which circuits contribute to an action, sensation, or symptom can help researchers decide what to study next. For example, an experiment in an animal model can test whether a group of cells influences a behaviour linked to a condition. A result can guide further investigation of that circuit, even if the eventual treatment uses a different method.

Clinical applications face their own questions. Researchers need a way to reach the right cells, deliver light to the right location, and establish safety and durability in people. A promising laboratory result answers a question about a model or a set of cells. Evidence from clinical research is needed to answer whether a proposed intervention helps patients. The prize recognises a method that changed how scientists investigate nervous systems.

## How do researchers know the light caused the change?

Imagine that a group of cells fires every time a light flashes. That pattern is promising, but it still leaves several explanations. The light may have opened the intended channels. The recording equipment might have picked up a disturbance at the moment the light switched on. In a behavioural study, an animal might respond to a visible glow or a sound from the equipment. A well-designed comparison helps distinguish these possibilities.

One useful comparison is between cells that contain the light-sensitive protein and similar cells that lack it. If the same flash produces the expected electrical response only in the first group, that strengthens the evidence that the protein matters. Researchers can also compare responses during pulses with activity between pulses. Looking at a series of trials helps establish whether the response follows the light consistently or appears only in an isolated recording. The most informative controls depend on what the study is measuring.

A voltage trace makes the logic easier to see. First mark the time when the light turns on. Then look at when the voltage starts to change and whether it crosses the threshold for a spike. If an illustrative trace shows a flash at 100 milliseconds and a spike at 107 milliseconds, the delay is 7 milliseconds. A single trace shows one response. Repeated measurements show whether the response usually occurs within a similar interval and how often it fails. These example numbers describe how to read a trace and do not reproduce a result from the 2005 paper.

## What can a circuit experiment prove?

Suppose researchers think a group of neurons helps an animal choose a direction. They could begin by recording the group during left and right turns. If the cells fire shortly before left turns, they have found a pattern. The next question is whether changing the group’s activity changes the likelihood of a left turn. An optogenetics experiment lets them ask that question during a defined part of the task.

Timing matters to the interpretation. If stimulation changes the choice only before a cue appears, the cells may play a different role from a group whose stimulation changes movement after the choice is made. The experiment can narrow the possibilities, but it still needs recordings and other evidence to distinguish roles such as preparing a movement, signalling a cue, and carrying out a decision.

Researchers can also ask whether the effect depends on the context. A group of cells might influence choices when a signal is hard to see and have little effect when the signal is obvious. In that case, a report that simply says the cells “control decisions” would lose the useful detail. Saying when the effect appeared helps other groups design comparisons and tells readers how far the result can be applied. That precision is one reason the method matters for understanding how circuits work.

## How to interpret an optogenetics experiment

Students can read an optogenetics paper by tracing four things: the cell population, the intervention, the readout, and the comparison group. If the intervention changes a behaviour, ask whether the study also verified that the targeted cells responded. If the readout is an electrical trace, identify whether the experiment measured a change in membrane voltage or a change in the number of spikes. Those are related measurements that answer different questions.

For researchers, the unit of analysis matters. Consider an illustrative experiment that measures 20 cells in each of five animals. The total is 100 cells, but it is still five animals. Cells from the same animal can share conditions that make their responses more alike. Before interpreting an uncertainty estimate, check whether the analysis accounts for this grouping. The same principle applies to repeated flashes delivered to one cell. More pulses can improve the measurement of that cell’s response, while measurements from more cells or animals help assess how widely the result applies.

Technical readers can use the same framework to compare figures across a paper. A cell recording can show that the intervention changed the intended signal. A circuit or behavioural result can show what happened under the test conditions. Comparing the two helps establish where the evidence is strongest and where the explanation still depends on assumptions.

## Conclusion

Hegemann, Nagel, Deisseroth, and their collaborators connected three discoveries. Algae use proteins that respond directly to light. One of those proteins, ChR2, allows a flash to change the electrical state of a neuron. Scientists can use that effect to test how selected cells contribute to a circuit. The lasting contribution is the ability to intervene at a chosen moment and measure what changes.
