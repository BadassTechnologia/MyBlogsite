A student can spend the day in lectures, reply to a group chat during dinner, and still feel like there is nobody they can call when they are upset. A remote worker can spend most of the day alone and feel well supported by two close friends. These examples help explain why the number of contacts on someone's phone and their experience of loneliness can tell different stories.

Young people report loneliness at comparatively high rates in some surveys. Explaining that pattern takes more care than calling Gen Z the loneliest generation. Age groups surveyed today differ in life stage, the opportunities they have to meet people, and the ways they experience online spaces. A one-time comparison cannot establish what those same generations felt at the same age. It can still help identify the kinds of support young people might need now.

## What is loneliness, and how do researchers measure it?

Loneliness is the distress someone can feel when their social relationships fall short of what they want. Social isolation describes more observable features, such as limited contact or having few ties. Social support addresses whether someone has access to help, practical or emotional, when they need it. The three experiences can overlap. A person can also have extensive contact and still feel unable to talk honestly with anyone in their circle.

Researchers may ask participants directly how often they feel lonely. Others use a scale that asks about experiences such as lacking companionship or feeling left out. The wording, response options, and threshold affect the estimate a survey reports. Someone may answer a question about feeling left out differently from a question that asks them to identify as lonely. That is one reason prevalence estimates from different surveys can vary without either survey being wrong.

It also matters how recently the questions were asked. A student who feels lonely on the first day of a new term may form close ties by the end of it. Someone who feels lonely most weeks for an extended period faces a different pattern. A survey with a short recall window can capture meaningful distress in both people, while a follow-up is needed to see which experience persists.

## What does the evidence say about younger people?

The 2025 WHO Commission on Social Connection report combined studies from multiple countries to estimate loneliness across age groups. Its pooled estimates were about 21 percent for adolescents ages 13 to 17 and 17 percent for young adults ages 18 to 29, compared with around 12 percent for adults 60 and older. These are global modeled estimates with uncertainty around them. The report presents intervals that capture that uncertainty, and the age bands cannot be substituted for precise birth cohorts.

A claim about age groups asks whether younger and older people report different experiences during the observed period. A generational claim asks whether people born in one era differed from earlier birth cohorts when each was at the same age. A historical claim asks whether changes at a particular time affected people across age groups. These questions are related, but a study designed to answer one can leave the others open.

Suppose a 22-year-old in a current survey reports less companionship than a 52-year-old in the same survey. One explanation might involve the uncertainty of leaving education or moving for work, things many people in the older group may have done decades earlier. Another might involve differences in the environments each generation grew up in. A shock during the survey period might also affect each group differently. Separating those effects requires comparable measurements over time and careful assumptions about which differences belong to each category.

The WHO results therefore support taking loneliness among adolescents and young adults seriously. They give less direct support to the claim that a named generation is uniquely lonely compared with every generation before it. For a local program, the more useful question might be which young people report unmet needs and what kinds of opportunities for connection are available where they live.

## Why can transitions disrupt belonging?

School and work transitions change the amount of repeated, low-pressure contact a person has. At school, classmates may see one another most days without having to organize a meeting. After a move, those incidental conversations can disappear. Arranging a new regular activity takes time, and the new acquaintance may live across town or work a different shift. A person can feel a real loss of connection during that gap even when they have good social skills and remain in touch with old friends.

Repeated contact can help a relationship develop because it creates small opportunities to learn whether another person follows through, shares interests, or responds well to a difficult conversation. Someone may initially want only a companion for a weekly activity. Over time, the same person may become a reliable source of emotional support. A large, one-off event can help people meet, while a recurring invitation gives them a clearer next chance to see each other.

The cost of showing up can influence which opportunities are realistic. A weekly activity may be technically available but require a bus trip home after service becomes infrequent. A student with paid work may have free time when an organized group is closed. Someone who recently moved may still depend on an old network that now lives hours away. Looking only at the number of clubs in a city can miss barriers that determine whether people can use them reliably.

For an institution, the design question starts with what happens after the first visit. Does a newcomer know where to go, how much the activity costs, and when they can return? Does anyone introduce them to another participant? When the location or time changes, how are people told? These details can affect whether a program offers a durable opportunity for relationships or only a momentary increase in contact.

## Does social media explain Gen Z loneliness?

The available evidence calls for separating what people do online from how many minutes they spend there. A 2025 cohort study led by Timothy Matthews analyzed 1,632 young adults in the United Kingdom, with an average age of 26 at the survey. The researchers reported an association between more time online overall and greater loneliness, while social media use in general did not show the same association in their data. Patterns varied across specific online experiences. The study used survey responses gathered in 2019 and 2020 and drew from a longitudinal twin cohort.

The distinction matters in everyday terms. Sending a message to a friend who lives far away, watching a live event with people you know, and scrolling through public posts can all happen on the same device. One activity can sustain an existing friendship, another can create a shared experience, and another can leave a person comparing their private life with someone else's highlights. Combining the three as screen time can hide which experience bears on the research question.

A correlation can also run in more than one direction. Someone who already feels disconnected may go online to look for company. A pattern of hostile or unwanted online interaction may leave someone feeling less connected afterward. Both pathways could exist in the same group of participants. A survey that measures online activity and loneliness at the same time makes it hard to decide which came first. Even data drawn from a longitudinal cohort need measurements at the right times to test that sequence.

For a specific group, ask what activity replaces what. If a student swaps an evening of disconnected browsing for a recurring chat with a friend, their total online time may stay similar while the social experience changes. If a digital group is their only accessible community, losing it can reduce support. A blanket claim about technology misses both situations and gives organizations little guidance about which environments to improve.

## How can loneliness persist even among friends?

A social network can offer conversation without offering the kind of connection someone wants. A new employee might enjoy team meetings and still miss friendships where they can talk about family or identity. Another person might have a supportive group that meets once a week but feel a gap in daily companionship. The same activity can help with one need and leave the other open.

Feeling excluded can also change expectations. After several invitations go unanswered, a person may become less willing to send another, even if there were ordinary reasons for the silence. Repeated experiences of rejection or discrimination can make caution understandable. An approach focused exclusively on confidence can miss the need for communities to change how they welcome and include people. An approach focused only on the environment can likewise overlook whether a person has safe ways to reconnect after an upsetting experience.

The kind of support matters. If someone mainly needs a person who will listen, a schedule full of networking events might give them limited relief. A peer group can help when people share a transition or an interest and can meet repeatedly. A structured conversation can help someone express a difficult experience, while a group activity can make it easier to spend time with others without talking about that experience immediately. Both can be useful for different reasons.

## What can schools, employers, and communities measure?

Start with the barrier a program aims to change. If new students do not know anyone on arrival, pair an initial introduction with a repeating activity. If evening transport limits attendance, trial an earlier meeting and see who can return. If remote employees want more informal contact, provide a regular space with time to talk without requiring them to disclose personal information. A concrete aim makes it possible to evaluate whether a change improved access.

Use attendance and experience together. A school might count first visits and return visits, then invite voluntary anonymous feedback on whether participants felt welcome and had an opportunity to talk with someone. A large crowd can look like success on opening day while quieter participants have little reason to return. A smaller recurring group might make more difference to its participants. Each design needs an outcome that reflects what the group is trying to accomplish.

Think about who is missing from the measurement. People with the least flexible shifts, the longest journeys, or low trust in an organization may be the least likely to complete a feedback form. Follow-up and multiple ways to participate can help an institution hear from people who are missed by a single event or survey. Avoid collecting more sensitive details than the program needs, and make clear whether results describe participation, felt belonging, or access to support.

## How should you read the next loneliness headline?

Check who was surveyed, how loneliness was measured, and which comparison the study can support. A global estimate for ages 18 to 29 can show that loneliness merits attention among young adults. A local longitudinal study can tell you how reports changed during a particular transition. An analysis of online activities can reveal differences that a general measure of screen time misses. Each kind of evidence is useful when the conclusion stays within its scope.

For practical decisions, start with the experience someone wants to change. Ask whether they want more opportunities to meet people, closer relationships, or better access to help. Then look for a change that addresses that specific need and has a realistic way to continue. A generation label can draw attention to loneliness. The evidence becomes actionable when it tells us more precisely who feels disconnected, under what conditions, and what they can access next.
